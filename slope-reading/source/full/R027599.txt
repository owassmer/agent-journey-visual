1. **The $499k withheld payable.** It stays withheld, gets netted in a settlement, or becomes due after a loss, which would hit $401k of cash. The timing is a window, because no schedule exists.
2. **Barfresh's claim.** Recovery, no recovery, or unresolved within the loan's life. The funder takes an unknown share. Following sound credit practice, no recovery counts as a source of repayment. A recovery only shows up as upside.

4. **The existing lender.** The facility's lien covers inventory, so Slope financing inventory needs the facility lender's consent or an intercreditor agreement. Its $1.4M of headroom also depends on eligible receivables.

## Each problem, and the remedy
**1. The signal never reaches the loan.**
- **Diagnosis:** two separate things are going on.
  - **The truthful result.** A $2M loan to a borrower with $27.7M of cash is safe, and no honest model should make it miss.
  - **The modelling flaw.** Collections continue until cash hits zero, which no real business does, and the loan-level output measures only the final failure.
- **Remedy (collection rule):** base payment capacity on each draw's own operating needs, not a fixed $7.3M reserve. Capacity is cash minus what that draw's own operating flows will need before the month's receipts come in. That is simply the lowest point of its cumulative operating flows for the rest of the month. No policy constant is needed, and it's more realistic.
- **Remedy (lender outputs):** add two quantities that move long before a missed payment:
  - **payment headroom** at each due date: cash above that operating need after the installment, as a distribution;
  - **backup liquidity**: available cash plus undrawn facility capacity, which the covenant channel (item 6) and a letter-of-credit bond both reduce.

  For ChromaDex, collections will probably still be full, and headroom and backup liquidity will show the erosion clearly. That is the honest lender story for a liquid borrower.
- **Recommendation:** add Synergy, a tight-cash case, as the second demonstration, where collections actually move with the same engine.

**2. Jev's own probabilities matter less than the structure.** Partly this is a framing error on my part, and partly it's scope.
- **The framing error.** The structure isn't "set by code". Jev's readings set it: who pays, "sought, not fixed", the stage, interest included. Flip the payer and the sign of everything flips. My test compared Jev's forecasts against a flat 50% while keeping Jev's readings, which undercounts Jev's contribution.
- **The honest part.** Jev's forecasts sit near 50/50 (56%, 58%, 24%, 69%) because the evidence genuinely doesn't settle these court events. Output close to "flat" is Jev reporting real uncertainty, not a defect.
- **The scope part.** One large amount and a loan that nothing can move leaves little leverage for probability detail. The channels in item 6 add third-party *decisions* (will the lender waive the covenant? will the appeal continue to drive legal spend?), where evidence can move probabilities materially.
- **Remedy:** show the contribution honestly as a three-step breakdown, using the existing override mechanism:
  1. bank data only;
  2. plus Jev's readings, with neutral forecasts;
  3. plus Jev's forecasts.

  That's one line on the page and directly tests your thesis. I would not add nodes just to make Jev "matter more"; add them only because they are real channels.

**3. The California waiver deleted the branch that actually happened.**

Everything else, including waivers, becomes evidence for the forecasts. This removes the constraint mechanism entirely, a simplification that also closes off the critical error of a reading deleting a feasible path. Jev's appeal forecast for California would then see the waiver and weigh it.
