246	### The missing distinction about “code owns the weights”
247	
248	Several different things were being called *weights*:
249	
250	- **Probabilities supplied by Jev** over a precisely defined answer space.
251	- **Mathematical composition** of those probabilities.
252	- **Coefficients describing how one factor predicts another outcome.**
253	- **Policy preferences** about which outcomes a lender accepts.
254	
255	Those are separate objects. Saying that code performs the computation does **not** require a developer to invent coefficients that replace Jev’s judgments.
256	
257	For example, if a well-defined Jev question produces probabilities over economically meaningful states, code can calculate the cash outcome under each state and preserve the resulting mixture:
258	
259	\[
260	P(\text{cash outcome}\mid E)
261	=
262	\sum_s P(\text{cash outcome}\mid s)\,P(s\mid E).
263	\]
264	
265	Here, \(E\) is the evidence and \(s\) is a modeled state. The probabilities can come from Jev; the cash consequences come from the financial model. No additional factor-importance coefficient is inherently necessary.
266	
267	Likewise, a sufficiently narrow, testable conditional forecast can obtain its probability directly from Jev. Atomicity means a clearly defined judgment with sufficient relevant context. It need not mean restricting Jev to recognizing words in an isolated sentence.
268	
269	**There remains a real distinction between targets:** the probability that a passage expresses an intention to appeal is different from the probability that an appeal will occur. If the model moves between those targets, that relationship needs to be supplied and evaluated—through a suitable Jev question, empirical modeling, or an explicit assumption.
270	
271	This is where I retain a narrower version of the caveat. Native probabilities are usable signals; their calibration for a particular forecasting task and their composition into a joint model remain empirical questions. That does not require arbitrary hand-written factor weights.
